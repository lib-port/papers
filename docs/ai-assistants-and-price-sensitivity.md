# AI Assistants, Price Sensitivity and the Future of Financial Pricing

## Executive summary

Widespread use of AI assistants could make customers more sensitive to the full cost of financial products. AI assistants could interpret contracts, compare suitable offers and monitor opportunities to renegotiate or switch. This would challenge pricing practices that depend on limited attention, difficult comparisons and customer inertia.

The likely change concerns how providers earn their margins. Funding costs, credit losses, insurance claims and capital requirements would continue to matter. Margins sustained by obscurity would face greater pressure, while demonstrable benefits could command a premium. This paper develops that conditional argument through hypothetical Australian home loans and insurance policies.

## Why AI could increase price sensitivity

Price sensitivity describes how strongly purchasing decisions respond to price differences. For an individual provider, customers become more price sensitive when they can identify and obtain suitable alternatives. Greater willingness to change lenders need not mean less demand for borrowing overall.

Financial comparisons require time, expertise and repeated attention. A customer must understand fees and conditions, establish eligibility and decide whether savings justify the disruption of switching. An assistant could spread this work across many products and revisit it when circumstances change. A small saving that once seemed insufficient to justify an afternoon of research could become worth pursuing.

This extends an established mechanism. Brown and Goolsbee found that internet comparison shopping reduced prices in the US term life insurance market ([2002](https://www.nber.org/papers/w7996)). Their evidence concerns an earlier technology and market, rather than AI in Australia. Gabaix and Laibson explain how competition can nevertheless sustain hidden charges when some consumers overlook them ([2006](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~xgabaix/papers/shrouded.pdf)). AI assistants could weaken that foundation by making overlooked terms salient.

The distinctive possibility is continuous, personalised comparison. An AI assistant could assess a contract against a household's circumstances and monitor it throughout ownership. Widespread adoption would then change providers' expectations about how many customers will notice and respond to an unattractive offer. Increased sensitivity could therefore affect pricing even before customers switch.

This need not reflect changed preferences. A customer may always have valued saving money but lacked the capacity to act. Where alternatives are genuinely substitutable and costs and quality are comparable, more responsive demand constrains the markup a provider can sustain. Reliable contract information, knowledge of customer preferences and authority to act would determine how far AI assistance translates into effective competition.

## Australian home loans: beyond the advertised rate

Consider two hypothetical A$400,000 home loans with 30-year terms and monthly principal-and-interest repayments. Loan A charges 6.0% annually and an A$2,000 establishment fee paid separately. Loan B charges 6.1% with no establishment fee. Assume both rates remain unchanged for two years, no offset balances or extra repayments, and no other fees.

Under monthly amortisation, nominal interest plus the establishment fee over those two years is approximately A$49,430 for A and A$48,231 for B. The higher-rate loan costs about A$1,199 less over that horizon. This comparison accounts for principal repayment through the amortisation calculation. Comparing monthly payments alone would miss the different remaining balances.

Australia already has comparison rates combining interest and most fees. AI's potential contribution is to apply the comparison to the customer's actual circumstances. [Moneysmart's home-loan guidance](https://moneysmart.gov.au/home-loans/choosing-a-home-loan) also highlights features and conditions requiring separate attention. An AI assistant could model likely offset balances, a shorter ownership period, introductory-rate expiry and applicable break costs. Those assumptions could change the preferred loan.

The ACCC's [2020 home-loan inquiry](https://www.accc.gov.au/media-release/home-loan-borrowers-missing-out-on-significant-savings-by-not-switching) found that many borrowers with older loans paid more than borrowers with newer loans and identified barriers to switching. If AI assistants make reviewing and challenging those differences routine, lenders would have less scope to recover acquisition discounts through later customer inattention. Approval requirements, refinancing costs and borrowers' changing circumstances would still restrict movement.

## Australian insurance: sensitivity to price and protection

Insurance requires comparison of both payments and contingent protection. Suppose two hypothetical home-and-contents policies have identical sums insured and coverage. Policy A has an annual premium of A$2,000 and an A$2,000 excess; Policy B charges A$2,300 with an A$500 excess. Assume one applicable excess and no other charges.

With no claim, A costs A$300 less. For a covered A$20,000 loss, premium plus excess totals A$4,000 under A and A$2,800 under B. Neither scenario establishes the better policy without considering claim likelihood, the household's ability to meet the excess and its tolerance of losses.

Actual policies introduce further differences. An AI assistant would need to compare Product Disclosure Statements and policy schedules for flood cover, exclusions, limits and adequate sums insured. [Moneysmart](https://moneysmart.gov.au/home-insurance/choosing-home-insurance) emphasises these distinctions. A household might rationally pay more after discovering that the cheaper policy leaves a consequential exposure uninsured.

Renewal pricing also offers scope for intervention. In its [2026 car-insurance research](https://www.asic.gov.au/about-asic/news-centre/find-a-media-release/2026-releases/26-189mr-consumers-left-in-the-dark-about-rising-car-insurance-premiums-asic-warns), ASIC reported that some customers who contacted their insurer obtained lower premiums without changing cover. This suggests an opportunity for AI assisted negotiation to result in material reductions in premiums across the market.

Insurance costs also depend on who buys. Research by [Einav and Finkelstein](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/jep.25.1.115) shows why customer selection matters. If AI assisted shopping changes the risk mix of an insurance pool, expected claims and sustainable premiums can change too. Greater price sensitivity alone cannot determine the outcome.

## How pricing models would change

These overlapping approaches would face different pressures:

| Pricing approach | Existing commercial logic | Likely response to widespread AI assistance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Risk-based and cost-plus pricing | Recover funding, expected losses or claims, operating costs and capital costs, with a margin. | More comparable offers constrain discretionary margins; underlying risk costs remain. |
| Introductory and renewal pricing | Accept a lower initial margin in anticipation of later profits from retained customers. | Monitor profitability over shorter customer relationships and offer competitive ongoing terms. |
| Bundles and ancillary charges | Earn revenue across a package of core products, features and fees. | Make the value of each component clearer as AI assistants compare the full package. |
| Personalised and dynamic pricing | Adjust offers to estimated willingness to pay, competitive conditions and customer behaviour. | Respond to informed negotiation, while potentially using richer data to discriminate more precisely. |
| Value-based pricing | Charge for benefits customers value beyond the basic product. | Substantiate premiums through useful flexibility, suitable cover and dependable service. |

Removing overlooked charges could increase upfront prices where those charges previously subsidised acquisition. Competitive pressure could redistribute payments across the contract as well as reduce margins. Providers would need to distinguish profitable differentiation from revenue that depends on customers overlooking its cost.

Pricing teams would also need to separate risk differences from differences in shopping behaviour. Historical renewal patterns may overstate future retention if AI assistants are used to routinely test alternatives. Models of price responsiveness, expected customer tenure and acquisition payback would need recalibration. Lending and insurance would retain their specialised risk models, while the commercial assumptions layered onto those models could change substantially.

## Conditions and strategic implications

The argument assumes capable AI assistants acting in customers' interests. Incomplete market coverage, misunderstood exclusions or commission-driven recommendations could undermine it. Concentrated AI assistant platforms might collect fees or control access to customers, transferring some existing margins to intermediaries. Sellers could also use AI to refine offers and infer willingness to pay.

Better information about product fit can strengthen differentiation. A [2026 theoretical working paper on agentic markets](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.25893) distinguishes cheaper search from more informative search and shows that the latter can soften competition under its assumptions. Improved understanding therefore need not always increase price sensitivity.

Providers should assess which margins survive informed and repeated comparison. Clear contract information, credible evidence of service quality and competitively priced ongoing relationships would become more valuable. Where AI assistants reliably reduce the effort of acting on price differences, sustainable returns would depend increasingly on efficient delivery and benefits customers can recognise and verify.
